Blue Light Health Parent Quiz Strategy & Voice
Parent-facing version of the Tech Loop quiz | v1 foundation
The Product Shift
The adult quiz asks: What loop am I in? The parent quiz asks: What is happening in our home, why are screens becoming the center of the relationship, and when does this need more than another rule?
Core idea: The parent quiz should not make a parent think, My kid is broken. It should make them think, This has become a family pattern, and we probably need support with the loop underneath it.

Quiz Purpose
Make the parent feel seen. The quiz should name the exhaustion, worry, guilt, and grief of feeling like screens are taking over the relationship.
Produce a formulation. The output should capture Child Tech Loop x Family Screen Pattern x Cost x Support Urgency.
Create a therapy-ready bridge. The result should move parents from stricter-rule thinking toward support for regulation, conflict, avoidance, belonging, or reward loops.
Act as a data instrument. The backend should save structured tags that can later inform clinical intake, ad segmentation, product learning, and follow-up messaging.
The Parent-Facing Promise
A short quiz that helps parents understand what screens may be doing for their child, what pattern the family may be stuck in, and what kind of support could actually help.
Do not lead with addiction. Lead with recognition: I miss my kid. I hate being the phone police. I can tell something is bigger than screen time, but every conversation turns into a fight.
What This Is Not
Not a diagnosis.
Not a screen-time calculator.
Not a parent-blame tool.
Not a claim that every child needs therapy.
Not a punishment pathway for kids who like games, social media, fandom, creators, AI, or online friends.
Not a generic advice quiz that ends with make a schedule and use parental controls.
Primary Parent Segments
Segment
Private parent thought
The Exhausted Enforcer
Screens have become the argument they are tired of having. They want limits, but every limit becomes a fight.
The Worried Observer
The child is not always explosive. They are quieter, more withdrawn, less present, and harder to reach.
The Sleep Spiral Parent
The parent sees bedtime, mornings, mood, and school starting to bend around late-night use.
The Social Pressure Parent
The parent can tell the child's mood is attached to something in a group chat, feed, streak, post, or social world they cannot fully see.
The Gaming or Online World Parent
The parent sees games, servers, fandoms, or online worlds becoming the main place the child feels alive, capable, or known.
The Reward-Risk Parent
The parent is worried about in-game spending, loot boxes, betting, trading, marketplace loops, secrecy, or money becoming part of the tech pattern.
The Burned-Out Peacekeeper
The parent gives in more than they want because they cannot handle another fight, and then feels guilty afterward.

Voice Rules
Use
Avoid
Scene over survey: Which sentence sounds most like your house?
Clinical screeners: frequency-first language that feels cold.
Parent private monologue: I hate being the phone police.
Accusation: You let screens take over.
Compassionate child framing: Screens may be doing something for them.
Child-blaming: They are lazy, addicted, manipulative, or defiant.
Honest cost: Sleep, trust, mood, school, family energy.
Moral panic: Screens are destroying your child.
Therapy as relief: support for the loop underneath.
Therapy as threat: Your child needs help because they cannot control themselves.
Careful uncertainty: may, can, often, could.
False certainty: This proves your child has a disorder.

Parent Quiz Formulation Model
Parent result = Child Tech Loop x Family Screen Pattern x Cost x Support Urgency.
Child Tech Loop: What screens appear to be doing for the child: soothing, belonging, checking, escape, competence, reward, stimulation, or social comparison.
Family Screen Pattern: What happens between parent and child: battle, negotiation, detective work, shutdown, peacekeeping, or collaborative limits.
Cost: Where the pattern is showing up: sleep, school, mood, family conflict, offline life, self-image, money, secrecy, or parent burnout.
Support Urgency: How much this has moved beyond ordinary tech tension: duration, failed rules, loss of control, concealment, impairment, money risk, or safety concerns.
Result Page Shape
Result headline: Your child's screen pattern may be The Nighttime Off-Switch.
Recognition line: The strongest sentence. It should sound like the parent's private thought, not a clinical summary.
What you may be seeing: concrete behaviors at home.
What screens may be doing for your child: the emotional job without over-explaining.
The family pattern: how the parent-child loop keeps repeating.
What it may be costing: honest but not shamey.
What helps at home: one to three practical shifts, not simplistic hacks.
Why support may help: therapy as a way to work on the loop underneath screens.
CTA: Book a consult, talk with a therapist, or get a plan for your family.
Therapy CTA Language
The result should make therapy feel reasonable, relieving, and early enough. It should not make the parent feel scared into action.
This is the kind of pattern that often needs more than another screen-time rule.
You do not need to wait until things are extreme to get support.
Therapy is not a punishment for screen use. It is support for the regulation, avoidance, conflict, or connection pattern underneath it.
A therapist can help your child build other ways to regulate, reconnect, tolerate limits, and rebuild trust at home.
A parent consult can help you stop carrying this alone and make a plan that does not turn every limit into a fight.
Ad and Marketing Guardrails
Do not write ads that imply a specific viewer has a mental health condition, addiction, or a child with a disorder.
Avoid direct callouts like: Is your teen addicted to their phone? Are you worried your child has anxiety? Your child needs therapy.
Use broader, observable family language: Screen-time arguments, bedtime battles, modern family tech patterns, and helping parents understand the pattern.
Keep the ad promise simple: Take the quiz to understand the screen pattern showing up at home.
Do not ask for health information in lead forms. The quiz can collect structured data on behavior and family patterns, with clear consent and privacy language.
Ethical Guardrails
Parent report is partial. The result should say what may be happening, not claim to know the child's inner life with certainty.
Do not pathologize normal adolescent privacy, identity exploration, fandom, gaming, online friendship, queer or gender exploration, creativity, or social media use.
Do not make every result severe. A low-support branch should exist for normal tech tension.
If a parent reports safety concerns, exploitation, self-harm content, violence, abuse, or dangerous contact, route to immediate professional or emergency support instead of a standard marketing result.
The quiz should be clear that it is educational and not medical advice, diagnosis, or crisis care.

One-Sentence Creative Brief
Creative brief: Build a short parent quiz that feels like a private mirror for screen-time stress at home, identifies the child tech loop and family pattern, and makes getting support feel like a sane next step instead of a dramatic last resort.
